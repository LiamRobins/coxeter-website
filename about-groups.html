About the Groups

What is a regular polytope?

A polytope is a shape with flat sides. A regular polytope is a polytope whose facets (sides) are all congruent, and adjacent facets meet at congruent angles.

In 0 dimensions, there is exactly one regular polytope, a single point.

In 1 dimension, there is exactly one regular polytope, a line segment.

In 2 dimensions, there are infinitely many regular polytopes, and they are the regular polygons (equilateral triangle, square, regular pentagon, etc.).

In 3 dimensions, there are exactly five regular polytopes, and they are the five Platonic solids (tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, icosahedron, and dodecahedron).

What are the reflections and chambers of a regular polytope?

A reflective symmetry of a polytope is a way to fold it along a flat divider so that the two halves match exactly. The divider itself is called a reflective hyperplane. Any reflective hyperplane of an $n$-dimensional shape centered at the origin will be an $(n-1)$-dimensional subspace: a point in 1 dimension, a line in 2 dimensions, a plane in 3 dimensions, and so on.

For instance, an equilateral triangle has three reflective symmetries, one for each line through a vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side. A square has four: two diagonals, and two perpendicular bisectors of opposite sides. A regular tetrahedron has six, one for each plane through an edge that bisects the opposite edge.

Drawing all of a polytope's reflective hyperplanes at once carves the polytope into smaller congruent pieces, called reflective chambers (or just chambers for short). Reflecting any chamber across a symmetric hyperplane yields another chamber. The three symmetries of the equilateral triangle produce 6 chambers; the four symmetries of the square produce 8 chambers; the six symmetries of the tetrahedron produce 24 chambers.

What is a Coxeter group?

Coxeter groups were first described in 1934 by the British geometer Harold Scott MacDonald Coxeter.

A Coxeter group is a group $W$ specified by a finite set of generators $S = \{s_1, \dots, s_n\}$ together with relations of the form $(s_i s_j)^{m_{ij}} = e$, where:

  • $m_{ii} = 1$ for all $i$, so each generator is its own inverse ($s_i^2 = e$);
  • $m_{ij} = m_{ji} \in \{2, 3, 4, \dots\} \cup \{\infty\}$ for $i \neq j$, with $m_{ij} = \infty$ meaning no relation is imposed on $s_i s_j$.

The generators in $S$ are called simple reflections. The standard example is the symmetric group $S_n$, generated by the adjacent transpositions $s_i = (i, i+1)$ for $i = 1, \dots, n-1$.

Reflections.

The set of reflections of $W$ is the set of all conjugates of the simple reflections: \[ T = \{ w s w^{-1} : w \in W, \ s \in S \}. \] Every simple reflection is a reflection, so $S \subseteq T$, but in general $T$ is larger. In $S_n$, $S$ consists of the $(n-1)$ adjacent transpositions, while $T$ is the set of all $\binom{n}{2}$ transpositions.

Length.

The length of $w \in W$, written $\ell(w)$, is the smallest integer $k$ such that $w$ can be written as a product $s_{i_1} s_{i_2} \cdots s_{i_k}$ of simple reflections. Such a minimal expression is called a reduced expression for $w$. The identity has length $0$, and each simple reflection has length $1$.

Inversions.

Choose $w \in W$. An inversion of $w$ is a reflection $t \in T$ such that $\ell(tw) < \ell(w)$; in other words, a reflection brings $w$ closer to the identity. The collection of all inversions of $w$ is its inversion arrangement: \[ \mathrm{Inv}(w) = \{ t \in T : \ell(tw) < \ell(w) \}. \] A foundational theorem states that $|\mathrm{Inv}(w)| = \ell(w)$ in any Coxeter group: the number of inversions of an element equals its length.

Inversion sequences.

Take $w \in W$, and take $t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_k \in T$ with the property that $t_i \in \mathrm{Inv}(t_{i-1} t_{i-2} \ldots w)$ for each $i$. Then, we say that $(t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_k)$ is an inversion sequence from $w$ to $t_k t_{k-1} \ldots t_1 w$.

Bruhat intervals.

Take $w_1, w_2 \in W$. We say that $w_2$ is in the Bruhat interval of $w_1$, denoted $w_2 \in \Bruhat(w_1)$, if there exists an inversion sequence from $w_1$ to $w_2$.

It can be seen that the Bruhat intervals of a Coxeter group $W$ form a partial ordering on that group. Meaning, for any $w_1, w_2, w_3 \in W$, the following conditions are satisfied:

  • Reflexivity: $w_1 \in \Bruhat(w_1)$ — simply take the null sequence, $()$.
  • Anti-symmetry: If $w_1 \in \Bruhat(w_2)$ and $w_2 \in \Bruhat(w_1)$, then $w_1 = w_2$.
  • Transitivity: If $w_1 \in \Bruhat(w_2)$ and $w_2 \in \Bruhat(w_3)$, then $w_1 \in \Bruhat(w_3)$.

How can you geometrically represent finite Coxeter groups?

There is a direct mapping between each finite Coxeter group and a regular polytope in Euclidean space. Under this correspondence:

  • The reflections (the set $T$) correspond bijectively to the reflective hyperplanes of the polytope.
  • The group elements correspond bijectively to the chambers carved out by those hyperplanes.
  • The length $\ell(w)$ equals the minimum number of hyperplanes one must cross when walking from a fixed base chamber to the chamber labeled by $w$.

For example, $S_n$ is the symmetry group of the regular $n$-vertex simplex. Its $n!$ elements correspond to the $n!$ chambers carved out by the $\binom{n}{2}$ reflective hyperplanes, one for each transposition.

(Note that this is not a perfect bijection: dual polytopes share a symmetry group, so for example the cube and octahedron both yield the Coxeter group $B_3$.)
